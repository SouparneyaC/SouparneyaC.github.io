# MATH496 — Understanding the Riemann Hypothesis

## Overview

MATH496, "Understanding the Riemann Hypothesis," Spring 2026, a MURL (MSCS Undergraduate Research Laboratory) research seminar supervised by Professor Evangelos Kobotis, 2 credit hours, prerequisite MATH181. No exams. Grading runs entirely on research meetings with the instructor and a collaboratively-produced poster at the end of the semester, ten weeks of actual research starting in week two, the rest of the semester spent on review and poster preparation.

## What I actually built

With research partner Paul Valiusis, a poster covering five sections: the Prime Number Theorem and its equivalent Chebyshev-function formulations, the complex analysis machinery needed to state the Hypothesis rigorously, analytic continuation and the residue theorem as the bridge to extending the zeta function, the Hypothesis itself, that every nontrivial zero of \(\zeta(s)\) in the critical strip has real part exactly \(\tfrac12\), and seven listed equivalent statements.

A separate personal synthesis document works through six of those equivalences in more depth, each restating the same underlying claim in a different branch of mathematics: the Prime Number Theorem's error term never growing worse than \(O(\sqrt{x}\log x)\); Robin's Inequality, a purely arithmetic bound with no complex analysis at all, where a single counterexample integer would disprove the Hypothesis outright; the Liouville function's cumulative sum behaving like a fair-coin random walk; Xian-Jin Li's criterion, converting the zero-location question into a sequence of non-negativity conditions; a divisibility-matrix determinant bound; and the de Bruijn-Newman constant, the equivalence connecting the Hypothesis to the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator, a spectral, quantum-mechanics-adjacent approach.

## Performance

The poster now credited on this site isn't the first one found under this class's name. An earlier pass located a Riemann Hypothesis poster filed inside the course folder and treated it as the deliverable, until direct comparison showed it belonged to a prior semester's team, kept as reference material rather than as the actual submission. The real poster turned up only after a targeted search of the Downloads folder more broadly, loose at the root rather than filed anywhere near the rest of the course's material. Separately, a personal library of nine Riemann-Hypothesis-specific papers sat in two generic, numerically-named dump folders, unconnected by filename or location to this branch at all; three of the nine turned out to be the three volumes of Kevin Broughan's *Equivalents of the Riemann Hypothesis*, and a fourth is Sabihi's paper on Robin's inequality and the Lagarias criterion, the same inequality named directly in the personal synthesis document. No citation trail proves the write-up was drafted from these specific papers rather than lecture material or the research meetings themselves, a distinction left unresolved rather than assumed either way.

## Where this shows up elsewhere
