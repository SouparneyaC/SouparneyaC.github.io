# FIN456 — Advanced Investment Management

> a report recommending ConocoPhillips cited a company's own announced 20 to 25 percent workforce cut in its sources, then never addressed it in the deck's own risk section.

## Overview

FIN 456/556, Advanced Investment Management, Fall 2025, Prof. John Wightkin. The class manages the Michael B. Mikhail Student Managed Investment Fund, an endowment of roughly $150,000 from the University of Illinois Foundation, not a simulation. Two sections share one portfolio; seven sector teams each own their sector's existing holdings and any new recommendations, report weekly, and present a formal buy or sell case that the whole class votes on before it goes to the custodian bank for execution.

## What the course covered

The semester runs fourteen classes across three phases. The first four are orientation: learning the fund's current holdings and sector exposures, forming teams, getting onboarded to a Bloomberg terminal, and starting to generate stock ideas. The next six build the actual analytical toolkit, one theme a week in order, ratio analysis, DCF modeling, factor and screening tools, risk modeling, performance attribution, and a macro overlay, each a Bloomberg-based skill applied directly to the sector team's own holdings rather than a generic exercise. The final four classes turn to synthesis: a peer review of every team's draft report, presentation coaching, then two full evenings of formal presentations across both sections.

Grading splits seven ways: the final investor presentation is worth the most at 30 percent, weekly sector updates and sector/individual deliverables are worth 15 percent each, and participation, quizzes, attendance, and peer reviews split the remaining 40 percent roughly evenly. A Bloomberg terminal login is required for the class; an optional Bloomberg Market Concepts certificate is available on top of it. The course's AI policy is specific rather than a blanket ban: AI is allowed for revising a student's own writing, for study aids, and for basic background research, banned outright on exams and quizzes, and any use elsewhere has to be logged and cited, with the syllabus stating directly that AI-generated content isn't a valid source for a factual claim.

## What I actually built

With three teammates, on the Energy sector team, I built the case to sell Ovintiv, the sector's worst performer at -29% total return, and replace it with ConocoPhillips. The valuation rested on two methods agreeing in direction: a DCF (3.5% five-year revenue growth, 2.0% terminal growth, 8.5% WACC) putting fair value at $118.40, +36.4% upside, and a weighted blend of five other methods, P/E, EV/EBITDA, Price/Book, Price/Cash Flow, a dividend discount model, landing at $116.40. ConocoPhillips was trading 11 to 15% below its own five-year average across every multiple checked, and 27% below its peer average, despite a stronger balance sheet (0.35 debt/equity against Ovintiv's 0.55) and nearly double the EBITDA margin of the integrated majors. The pitch modeled the swap's effect on the whole portfolio, not just the stock: beta down (1.38 to 1.25), volatility down, expected return and Sharpe ratio both up. Final call: buy, $105 target, +22% twelve-month upside.

Separately, the fund assigns rotating individual holding-analysis reports on stocks outside a student's own sector. Mine was on Lowe's, a Consumer Discretionary holding: a full Porter's Five Forces writeup, a quantitative snapshot (net margin near 9.9%, four of the last five quarters beating earnings estimates), and a HOLD recommendation, the valuation already reflects most of the Pro-contractor growth story, and near-term housing-market weakness caps the upside either way.

The syllabus also requires every student to read and critique every other sector team's report before the final vote, not just their own. My review of the Industrials team's Waste Management pitch praised a clear thesis backed by a competitor table, WM operating in all fifty states against narrower competitor footprints, 286 owned landfills against a rival's 208, a named $7.2 billion acquisition, and pushed back on specific weaknesses: a valuation section needing summary takeaways, repetitive rationale around the funding sale, and a risk section that needed a quantified impact or a named mitigation attached to each risk, the same bar my own team's deck was held to.

## Performance

The ConocoPhillips deck cites sixteen dated sources, SEC and investor filings, Reuters, Macrotrends, and every figure in it checks out against those sources. One gap in it: the sources include a Reuters report of ConocoPhillips announcing a 20-to-25% workforce cut, and the deck's own risk-factors section never addresses it. The Lowe's report closes the way good analysis should: naming an open question, how resilient the Pro-contractor segment actually is if housing stays weak, instead of closing the case tidier than the evidence supports.

It isn't something I built alone; it's a professional process I operated inside of, seven sector teams, a shared portfolio, a vote that actually moves money, a different kind of evidence for how I work under a process I didn't design myself.
