# STATS401 — Probability Theory

*A course entry within [Coursework](/coursework/).*

> a self-built solution catalogue for 86 exam-style questions was built without ever opening the answer key, every derivation independently checked against the exam's own structure instead.

## Overview

STATS401, Probability Theory, an eight-week summer 2026 course taught by Gregory Kyle Taylor, using Hogg, McKean, and Craig's textbook. The course runs foundational probability: sample spaces and Kolmogorov's axioms, random variables and moment generating functions, joint and conditional distributions, the full family of discrete and continuous distributions, Bernoulli through Poisson, Exponential through the t-distribution, and limiting theory culminating in the Central Limit Theorem.

## What the course covered

Grading: written homework 20 percent (lowest score dropped), two 60-minute exams at 25 percent each, and a cumulative two-hour final worth 30 percent. The eight-week schedule runs in two phases. The first six weeks build the distribution machinery from the ground up, probability spaces and set functions, random variables and their transformations, conditional distributions and independence, expectation and the correlation coefficient, then a full two-week block on the named special distributions before the second exam. The last two weeks shift entirely to limiting behavior: convergence in probability, then convergence in distribution, then the Central Limit Theorem, one lecture each, in that order, building the CLT as the destination of a three-step argument rather than introducing it on its own.

## What I actually built

A full self-made study system for the course: a page-indexed textbook locator, a lecture-by-lecture coverage map, and a fully solved 86-question exam-prep catalogue, built under one rule enforced throughout: the actual exam's answer key was never opened while building it. Every solution was derived independently and checked only against the exam's own structure, not against a stated answer.

## Performance

That rule is the same discipline good verification always comes down to: derive first, check against ground truth second, never the other way around. The same system also caught its own scheduling problem: the semester's actual exam dates drifted from the printed syllabus almost immediately, and the study system tracked the actual calendar rather than the stale one.
