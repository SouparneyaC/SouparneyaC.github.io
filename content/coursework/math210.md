# MATH210 — Calculus III

> only two of an estimated fifteen weeks' worksheets survive, and both turned up filed inside a different class's folder entirely.

## Overview

MATH210, Calculus III, Fall 2025, the third and final course in UIC's standard calculus sequence after Calculus I and II, shifting from single-variable analysis to vectors, multivariable functions, and the calculus built on top of them. A department-wide syllabus, shared across 21 parallel lecture sections; this section met MWF under Instructor Whyte. Grading: two midterms at 17 percent each, a final exam worth 36 percent, written homework 10 percent, online homework 10 percent, and quizzes 10 percent, each category dropping its lowest score or two.

## What the course covered

The first two weeks stay with vectors themselves: distance and the dot product in the plane and in space, then the cross product, torque, and the vector and parametric equations of lines and planes. Week three turns those vectors into functions of a single variable, tracking a point moving through space, and differentiates them to get velocity, acceleration, and speed directly from a position vector.

The middle of the course generalizes single-variable calculus to several variables at once: functions of two and three variables, level curves and surfaces, then partial derivatives, the gradient, and directional derivatives, building toward the first midterm. What follows immediately after uses that machinery for optimization, linear approximation, critical points, and the method of Lagrange multipliers for optimizing a function subject to a constraint, before the course turns to double and triple integrals, first over simple regions, then in polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates as the region gets less convenient.

The back half, after a second midterm, moves from integrating over regions to integrating along paths and across surfaces: line integrals of scalar functions and of vector fields, conservative fields and when a vector field has a potential function, then the course's own capstone, Green's Theorem, Stokes' Theorem, and the Divergence Theorem, three results that each relate an integral over a boundary to an integral over what it encloses, one dimension up from the last.

## What I actually built

Two worksheets survive locally, both misfiled inside a different class's folder rather than this one's own. The first works through simplifying a vector expression built from three arbitrary points, then finds the specific scalar value that makes two given vector combinations parallel. The second, matching the course's velocity-and-acceleration material, takes a position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t,\ 2\sin t,\ 5t\rangle\), finds velocity, acceleration, and speed at a specific instant, classifies the angle between velocity and acceleration by the sign of their dot product, and works the same idea in reverse: reconstructing a position function from a given velocity function and a starting point, one antiderivative at a time.
