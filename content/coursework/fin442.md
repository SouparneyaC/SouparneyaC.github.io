# FIN442 — International Corporate Financial Management

*A course entry within [Coursework](/coursework/).*

> a case built around Caterpillar's 2024 tariff forecast got the tariff itself right and the currency call backwards: the dollar hit a three-year low instead of the safe-haven strengthening the forecast expected.

## Overview

FIN442/542, International Corporate Financial Management, Summer 2025, Professor Tor-Erik Bakke. The course's own objective: financial decision-making inside a multinational firm, valuing it, valuing its investment opportunities, and hedging its risks, with explicit attention to where the standard domestic models stop working. Grading: participation 8 percent, six cases at 5 percent each, five quizzes at 5 percent each, and a final exam worth 37 percent. AI use is explicitly permitted on assignments with a required disclosure statement, and explicitly prohibited on exams.

## What the course covered

Eleven units carry the arc, foreign exchange markets and purchasing power parity first, then the three types of currency exposure taught as a deliberate contrast set: transaction exposure, mechanical and contractual, the easiest to hedge; translation exposure, an accounting effect with no direct cash impact; and operating exposure, a structural competitiveness risk with no financial instrument that hedges it directly. Hedging strategy follows, both financial, forwards, options, swaps, and operational, supply-chain diversification, natural hedging, contract design. The back half moves through capital structure and capital budgeting for global firms, equity valuation taught through several competing methods rather than one canonical approach, FDI and cross-border M&A under political and expropriation risk, corporate governance through an activist-investor lens, and closes with international tax and transfer pricing and international portfolio diversification. Two further units, the efficient market hypothesis and options pricing, have their own lecture decks but never appear in the syllabus's own numbered topic list, bridging material rather than a graded unit of its own.

## What I actually built

Case 1, individual, put me in Caterpillar's CEO seat in late 2024, responding to an analyst's tariff-and-currency forecast against the company's own risk profile, operations in more than 180 countries, roughly 60 percent of sales outside the U.S. The retrospective evaluation piece caught the forecast's mixed record directly: it got the tariff right, an actual 10 percent global tariff followed in April 2025, and got the currency call backwards, expecting a safe-haven dollar rally that never came, the dollar instead hit a three-year low amid reports the administration was considering replacing the Fed chair. The hedging strategy paired financial instruments, forwards, options, cross-currency swaps, interest-rate swaps for the financing arm's funding cost, with operational hedges already underway, supply-chain diversification, local sourcing and selling, and contract provisions for mid-stream price revision.

Case 2, a group submission with two teammates, applied the course's three-exposure framework to General Motors' yen exposure around 2000: a $900 million transaction exposure already covered by GM's formal hedging policy, a translation exposure from equity stakes in Japanese firms, a non-cash effect only relevant if the holdings were sold, and a $500 million yen-denominated financing exposure whose direction runs opposite the other two, making the net effect of a currency move ambiguous. The submission explains why GM's own risk officer worried more about the exposure type with no direct hedge available than the two that were fully covered: a weaker yen lowers Japanese competitors' production costs, worth more than $400 million in added Japanese operating profit per one-yen depreciation in the case's own numbers, a structural pricing threat rather than a cash-flow line item, backed by GM's own $1,969 average per-vehicle incentive spend against lower Japanese incentive spending as concrete evidence of the margin pressure.
