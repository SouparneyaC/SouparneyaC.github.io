# FIN480 — Securities Markets and High-Frequency Trading

> a real day of MSFT tick data, cleaned by hand, then turned into a plan for slicing a 1.5-million-share order into thirty-minute bins.

## Overview

FIN480/580, Securities Markets and High-Frequency Trading, Spring 2025, Professor Dermot Murphy. A hands-on trading-simulation course built around the Rotman Interactive Trader platform, not a lecture-and-exam course, students trade against each other and against bots in live, instructor-run simulations covering agency trading, liability trading, market making, and algorithmic strategies. Textbook: Joel Hasbrouck's own teaching notes, *Securities Trading: Procedures and Principles*. Grading: assignments 20 percent, participation 10 percent, a closed-book midterm 35 percent, and a closed-book final 35 percent. I withdrew partway through the semester; real course material survives through week ten, meaning substantial engagement happened before the withdrawal, not an early drop.

## What the course covered

The material through week ten runs from market structure into live practice. Limit order markets and how trading fragments across multiple venues open the course, then the Rotman Interactive Trader itself, introduced through a "trading as a principal" simulation, followed by a full agency-trading day covering VWAP execution strategies and price impact. Dealer markets and transaction cost analysis follow, then auctions and dark markets, before a first trading day built around liability trading in illiquid markets and dynamic order arrival, closing out the material tested on the midterm. The material that survives past the midterm covers algorithmic trading, arbitrage and market-making strategies run live in the simulator, and pricing, fees, and Reg NMS, the regulatory structure behind the market design covered earlier.

## What I actually built

The first assignment works with a real day of MSFT trade-and-quote data, January 13, 2003: clean it by removing cancelled, corrected, and unusually-conditioned trades along with anything outside regular trading hours, then compute the volume-weighted and time-weighted average price for the day from what's left. The last part turns the analysis into a plan, given an order to buy 1.5 million shares the following day, how many shares to execute in each thirty-minute window, based on the day's own actual volume distribution.

## Performance

No submission for that assignment survives locally, consistent with a withdrawal partway through the course rather than a completed one. The material that does survive, weekly lecture decks through week ten, two sets of worked example solutions, and case files reaching into week ten specifically, reflects a course engaged with well past its earliest weeks. This entry exists on the site despite the withdrawal because completion isn't the bar for what's real coursework.
