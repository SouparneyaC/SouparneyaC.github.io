# England versus France, third place play-off

*A case study within [Crowd belief compression in a live forecasting competition](/research/crowd-belief-compression/).*

## Abstract

I priced the fifteen questions posed for the 2026 World Cup third place match between France and England, scored under the competition's relative Brier rule against a crowd of other forecasters. Point in time Elo ratings feed a Poisson goal rate regression whose scoreline grid answers every goal structure question, a hierarchical partial pooling model prices the team count questions, and player questions use a recency weighted rate model. Each component was validated out of sample before it was trusted: a walk forward backtest showed the goal model was well calibrated for match result but had no edge on total goals, a shadow comparison against the crowd confirmed the same weakness a second time, and a backtest of the player model exposed systematic overconfidence on high usage forwards. The operating rule that followed was simple: trust the model where it demonstrably beats the crowd, and defer to the sharp betting market everywhere it does not. The match has since settled, and I report it as a single out of sample test of the whole procedure. The submitted slate gained 100.63 relative Brier points; the questions on which the model had earned an edge carried the result, and the one weakness the validation had already identified, total scoring, cost the most, in a ten goal game that was the extreme version of the scenario the model was right to be uncertain about.

## Data

Elo ratings, built by replaying every 2026 result in chronological order so that the rating a team carries into a match reflects only results strictly before it, put France at 2196.4 and England at 2149.2 at kickoff, a 47.3 point difference in France's favor. Team event counts come from a unified panel of 460 team match rows, 204 from 2026 ESPN box scores and 256 from StatsBomb annotated 2018 and 2022 matches, with a source indicator absorbing the measurement gap between the two feeds. Player form for the three named forwards, Kane, Mbappé, and Dembélé, is drawn from their complete 2026 tournament logs, seven matches each.

A market's order book depth, the stake resting against a price, is not the same as its traded volume, the stake that has actually changed hands, and the gap matters for which prices can be trusted as anchors. The match result market (687 million in depth, 282,000 traded) and both teams to score (157 million depth, 6,600 traded) were both deep and heavily traded, reliable anchors. The nine or more corners market was deep but almost untraded (37 million depth, 73 traded), its tight quote a market maker's posting rather than a settled consensus. The twenty or more total shots market had no depth and no volume at all. A price was treated as a usable anchor only when both depth and traded volume were substantial; the rest were priced from the model and the crowd.

## Models

Goals are modeled as Poisson counts with a logged rate linear in the Elo difference, fitted by maximum likelihood on the 460 row panel:

\[
\log \mathbb{E}[\text{goals}] = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{home} + \beta_2\,\Delta\text{Elo} + \gamma\,\text{StatsBomb}
\]

with \(\beta_0 = 0.2189\), \(\beta_1 = 0.1454\) (zeroed at prediction since the venue was neutral), \(\beta_2 = 0.001710\) per Elo point (\(z = 8.5\)), and a StatsBomb source adjustment of \(\gamma = -0.0611\). At the match's 47.28 point Elo gap this gives \(\lambda_{\text{France}} = 1.35\) and \(\lambda_{\text{England}} = 1.15\), a combined 2.50 expected goals, roughly 0.9 goals below the 3.4 the betting market implied. That gap is not treated as a model defect: the Elo difference already conditions on the quality of today's specific opponent, while each side's high tournament scoring average was built against weaker group stage opposition, and an independent attack versus defence cross check returns a similar total. The market's higher figure most plausibly prices the open, low stakes character of a third place play-off, a factor no tournament trained rate model can see.

The independent Poisson joint distribution understates draws, so the four low score cells are corrected by a single dependence parameter, fitted at \(\rho = -0.06\), following Dixon and Coles (1997). Goals are close to equidispersed (variance to mean ratio 1.03), which supports the Poisson grid for goal structure questions, but corners (2.12), shots on target (1.82), and especially total shots (3.23) are substantially overdispersed, so a negative binomial marginal is used for those families instead. Each side had played only seven matches, too few to trust a raw per team average, so corner and shot on target rates come from a hierarchical Poisson model with a team random effect that shrinks each team's estimate toward the population mean. The shrinkage matters in practice: it raises France's shots on target rate to 4.55 and England's to 3.81, and in turn raises the price that both teams reach four shots on target from 0.26 under a plain fixed effect fit to 0.35. For the three named forwards, a scoring probability is built from each player's share of his team's own 2026 output, \(P(\text{involved}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda_p}\) with \(\lambda_p = s \cdot \lambda_{\text{team}}\): Kane's share of 6 goals in 14 tournament shots gives 0.39, Mbappé's 8 in 16 gives 0.49, and Dembélé's involvement share gives 0.45 for a score or assist question.

## Validation

Four checks tested whether each component held up on data it was not fit to, rather than trusting the in sample fit. A walk forward backtest across all 102 tournament matches, refitting at each match date on strictly prior data only, showed the goal model was a clear improvement on match result (Brier 0.192 against a 0.250 base rate) and well calibrated in the interior of the range, but had no edge on both teams to score (0.2475 against 0.2476) and was slightly worse than the base rate on total goals (0.2506 against 0.2465), the same pattern the goal total discrepancy above had already predicted. The hierarchical count model was validated the same way, per family, with a multiple testing correction across families: shots on target and corners both survived at \(p = 9.6\times10^{-5}\) and \(p = 0.030\), and their prices were the ones carried forward, while cards and offsides did not survive and were left to the crowd. The player model, tested on 1,016 player matches from 2018 and 2022, improved on a player's own prior scoring rate (Brier 0.104 against 0.119) but was poorly calibrated at the top of the range: where it predicted a scoring probability of 0.45 or higher, the realized rate was only about 0.25, systematic overconfidence for exactly the high usage forwards this match was pricing. Finally, a direct shadow comparison against the crowd on settled 2026 questions showed the model essentially level with the crowd on match result and both teams to score, but materially worse on total goals (0.261 against 0.225), a third independent signal that the model must not be allowed to drive the total goals questions.

## The final slate and result

The validation results set a single deference rule: let the model deviate from the crowd only in the families where it had demonstrated an edge, and anchor everything else to the market or the crowd. Thirteen of fifteen questions were submitted under this rule; the two shot count questions the validation had flagged as least reliable, each team reaching four shots on target and the match reaching twenty total shots, were held back entirely. The match settled France 4, England 6, ten goals in total, the direct opposite of the tight defensive game that would have vindicated the model's low expected total. The submitted slate gained 100.63 relative Brier points, and ten of the thirteen beat the crowd.

The points came from precisely the questions the validation had licensed a deviation on. Holding France below the crowd on the match result and on scoring first, both of which England's win settled in the model's favor, returned +29.22 and +17.68. The hierarchical corner reading, below the crowd and correct, returned +15.98. The Dembélé score or assist question, priced above a thin, one sided market offer on the judgment that his involvement rate did not justify the discount, returned +30.07, the single best result of the match. The largest loss fell exactly where the paper had already said the model was weakest: the any player scores twice question, priced low off a low expected goal total, lost 21.61 RBP when the ten goal game made a brace almost inevitable, and the Mbappé scoring question lost 9.52 to the same overconfidence shade the player backtest had already prescribed. The two questions held back entirely both resolved yes, exactly the shot count territory the validation had already marked as unreliable.

## Discussion

A single match cannot certify any one of these probabilities, and this paper does not claim it does. What it can show is a pattern: edge where the model had earned it through out of sample validation, loss concentrated exactly where that same validation had already flagged the model as weak. That is not a vindication of any individual number. It is the process behaving the way its own out of sample tests said it would, which is the only claim a single realized match can actually support.
